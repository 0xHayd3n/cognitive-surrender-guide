## Why the authors add a third system

Dual-process accounts distinguish relatively automatic, associative processing from effortful checking and rule-based thought. The paper surveys several versions of that distinction rather than treating Kahneman as its sole originator: Stanovich and West, Sloman, Epstein, Evans, and neuroscience work all appear in the background. These traditions differ in how they describe monitoring, capacity, and motivation. The paper also acknowledges criticism of sharply dividing cognition into two systems. Reading the survey as proof of a literal two-part brain would miss this qualification. (pp. 7–10.)

Shaw and Nave argue that familiar accounts underdescribe the active role of external AI. AI can offer answers, generate alternatives, and reshape when people check their own intuitions. They define System 3 through four properties: it is outside the biological nervous system, performs operations automatically, depends on data and training, and responds dynamically to people and contexts. The proposal expands the unit of analysis from an unaided person to a person interacting with external reasoning infrastructure. This is an explanatory framework, not a brain measurement. (pp. 10–13.)

Table 1 compares origin, speed, effort, accuracy, affect, ethics, and justification across the three systems. Treat its descriptions as theoretical characterizations. In particular, the table's suggestion that well-trained AI can be neutral or nonpartisan does not establish that current AI systems lack bias. The text itself recognizes training-distribution gaps, brittleness, and limited situational or experiential understanding. (p. 12.)

## Routes matter more than a new label

Figure 1 and Table 2 allow several routes to an answer. Intuition can produce a response quickly; conflict can recruit deliberation; deliberation can ask AI for support and integrate the answer; AI can also provide an output that receives very little checking. Hybrid routes include verifying an output, overriding it, rationalizing an answer afterward, or changing later intuitions. Autopilot is the proposed limiting case in which the stimulus reaches an AI-generated response without internal engagement. These are conceptual routes, not six observed experimental treatment arms. (pp. 14–16.)

An important nuance for comparison with System 0 arguments: Figure 1 explicitly allows System 3 to operate before internal cognition, and Table 2 includes autopilot. This paper's experiments nevertheless study discrete optional consultations. The model's breadth and the paradigm's measured behavior should be discussed separately.

## Surrender versus assistance

The authors distinguish **cognitive offloading**, where a person strategically delegates work while remaining engaged, from **cognitive surrender**, where external output is accepted with little critical evaluation. Following a correct answer does not by itself separate the two. One person may have checked it carefully; another may have accepted it immediately. Faulty advice is more diagnostic because it exposes cases where a response follows a wrong recommendation. Even then, the observable wrong answer does not reveal every intermediate mental step. (pp. 17–18; operational categories p. 44.)

Surrender is also not identical to poor performance. Deferring to a reliable specialist can be sensible. The concern is the loss of sensitivity to when that advice is wrong and the transfer of judgment without adequate scrutiny. The authors explicitly acknowledge that deference can be adaptive in some domains. (p. 18.)

## Neighboring concepts

The paper connects its proposal to extended-mind theory, distributed cognition, transactive memory, automation bias, and epistemic outsourcing. Its claimed addition is an account of real-time routing between internal thought and interactive external AI. Whether this warrants a distinct system, rather than extending existing models of advice taking and automation bias, remains a theoretical question. Web Appendix A is said to expand these comparisons but is absent from the attachment. (pp. 5, 13, 17.)

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning in this guide |
| --- | --- |
| System 1 | The paper's shorthand for intuitive, associative processing. |
| System 2 | The paper's shorthand for reflective, effortful processing. |
| System 3 | Proposed external artificial cognition. |
| AI-Assisted | AI is available, whether or not it is used on that trial. |
| AI-Accurate / AI-Faulty | Assigned recommendation quality, not observed human correctness. |
| Consultation / chat-engaged | Participant actually used the embedded assistant. |
| Override | Rejecting the AI recommendation; this need not produce a correct answer. |
| Thinking profile | A behavior-defined usage subgroup, not a randomized personality type. |
