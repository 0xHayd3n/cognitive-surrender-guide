# 4. Study 2: time pressure and selected usage profiles

Source: Study 2 pp. 27–33, Figures 5–6. [Corresponding public v1 PDF, p. 27](https://osf.io/download/9gxaq/#page=27) · [SSRN record](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6097646).

## What changes

Study 2 includes 485 participants: 138 from the laboratory and 347 from Prolific. Everyone has optional AI on six of seven trials; a randomly positioned Brain-Only probe supplies a within-session baseline. Participants are assigned either a 30-second item deadline (n=228) or untimed control (n=257). Timeouts count as incorrect. Confidence remains global; Trust in AI, NFC, and timed fluid intelligence follow the task. (pp. 27–28.)

The timer intervention is randomized. Usage is not. This matters because the paper's most vivid account of AI buffering time-pressure costs concerns behavior-defined AI-Users rather than a separately randomized group.

## Advice is still followed

When available, AI is consulted on 52.0% of trials overall (53.9% control; 49.9% timed). Conditional follow rates are 80.3% versus 67.4% for correct advice and 74.6% versus 72.3% for faulty advice, control versus timed. However, the Time Pressure × Trial Type interaction for following is not statistically significant: OR=0.61, 95% CI [0.34,1.10], p=.102. The result does not establish that time pressure reliably changes discrimination between correct and faulty recommendations. (p. 28.)

Opening chat predicts better accuracy when AI is accurate and worse when AI is faulty. This is consistent with advice influencing answers, but consultation is a participant choice; these associations should not be presented as the isolated causal effect of opening chat.

## Time pressure lowers performance

The Brain-Only probe shows 32.6% accuracy under pressure, 95% CI [26.5,38.8], versus 46.9% in control, 95% CI [40.7,53.1]. The reported contrast OR=0.53, 95% CI [0.36,0.79]. Across AI-available trials, accuracy is 38.9% timed, 95% CI [36.3,41.5], and 51.9% control, 95% CI [49.3,54.5]; OR=0.42, 95% CI [0.31,0.57]. (pp. 28–29.)

The full accuracy model, controlling for usage, finds no evidence of Time Pressure × Trial Type interaction: OR=0.98, 95% CI [0.63,1.53], p=.937. Thus the overall data do not show that AI removes the time-pressure penalty. They show a strong role for advice quality alongside a continuing timer cost. A nonsignificant interaction also does not prove exact equality of effects.

## Why the profile plots look striking

The authors classify 170 people as Independents (at most one consultation) and 315 as AI-Users (at least two). Independents' accuracy is 51.5% in control and 34.0% timed; assigned AI quality has no reliable effect within this low-use group. (p. 29.)

For AI-Users, correct-advice accuracy is 80.0% control and 71.3% timed, while faulty-advice accuracy is 20.4% and 12.1%. The accurate-versus-faulty contrast within this group has OR=40.9, 95% CI [26.1,64.1]. Figures 5–6 display these patterns. Correct advice can therefore leave frequent users doing better under pressure than people answering without useful assistance. It still does not eliminate their own timed-versus-untimed decrease. (pp. 29–31.)

This is the careful reading of “buffers”: correct AI can compensate for part of a constrained person's task difficulty in this setting, while faulty AI creates a severe vulnerability. It is not evidence that time pressure improves reliance or that everyone should consult AI under deadlines.

## Traits and interpretation

Trust in AI predicts greater use and following. NFC and fluid-intelligence associations are not uniformly reliable here; neither profile difference in fluid intelligence nor its following effect is significant. Some tests are described using trial-level observations, so readers should inspect the authors' exact models before equating every reported degree of freedom with participant counts. (pp. 31–32.)

The authors interpret pressure as suppressing deliberation. That is a process account of a timer-and-response pattern, not a direct measurement of System 2. The experiment's useful contribution is showing that strong dependence on recommendation quality remains under a meaningful constraint on response time.
