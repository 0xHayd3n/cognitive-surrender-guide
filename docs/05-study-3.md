# 5. Study 3: incentives plus feedback

Source: Study 3 pp. 33–40, Figures 7–9. [Corresponding public v1 PDF, p. 33](https://osf.io/download/9gxaq/#page=33) · [SSRN record](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6097646).

## The intervention is a bundle

The 450 Prolific participants are assigned to incentives plus feedback (n=238) or control (n=212). Each correct answer in the intervention earns $0.20 and an entry into a $20 lottery; item-level correctness feedback follows submission. Control has no bonus or feedback. AI access, assigned advice quality, and a randomly positioned Brain-Only probe otherwise follow Study 2. Confidence is now measured after each response and before feedback. (p. 33.)

This design tests the combined package. It cannot identify whether money, lottery entries, error feedback, or their combination produces a change. An intervention that both motivates accuracy and reveals mistakes is a stronger test than merely telling people to be careful, but it is not a component experiment.

## Selectivity improves among consulted trials

For correct recommendations, conditional following rises from 87.6% control, 95% CI [84.4,90.8], to 92.2% intervention, 95% CI [89.5,94.9]. For faulty recommendations, override rises from 20.0%, 95% CI [16.1,23.9], to 42.3%, 95% CI [37.4,47.2]; the override contrast OR=4.25, 95% CI [2.62,6.90]. The intervention × advice-quality interaction for following is significant: OR=8.19, 95% CI [4.13,16.27]. (pp. 33–35, Figure 7.)

The behavioral change is selective: people accept more good advice and reject more bad advice. Still, most consulted faulty recommendations are followed in the intervention. Increased override need not mean a correct replacement answer; those outcomes must be distinguished.

## Accuracy improves without erasing the gap

| Outcome | Control | Incentives plus feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brain-Only probe | 42.4%, CI [36.1,48.7] | 64.2%, CI [57.7,70.7] |
| All AI-available trials | 49.6%, CI [47.1,52.1] | 63.3%, CI [60.6,66.0] |
| AI-Accurate trials | 68.5%, CI [65.2,71.8] | 81.0%, CI [77.9,84.1] |
| AI-Faulty trials | 30.7%, CI [27.4,34.0] | 45.5%, CI [41.6,49.4] |

All intervals are author-reported 95% CIs. Sources: pp. 35–36, Figure 8 p. 37. Available-trial means include nonconsultation. The AI-trial overall intervention contrast is OR=2.54, 95% CI [1.80,3.56].

The full accuracy interaction between advice quality and intervention is not significant (p=.609). Do not confuse this with the significant conditional-following interaction: they use different outcomes and denominators. The package improves performance, including on faulty-advice trials, but recommendation quality remains a large source of variation.

## Confidence and profile analysis

Per-item confidence averages 82.2 on AI-available trials, 95% CI [81.3,83.1], versus 77.5 on the no-AI probe, 95% CI [75.1,79.9]. Confidence does not differ reliably by assigned advice quality (p=.682), and the intervention difference is not significant at .05 (p=.066). Nonetheless, confidence retains some diagnostic value: within the authors' model, a 10-point confidence increase is associated with OR=1.22 for correctness, 95% CI [1.16,1.29]. Elevated confidence and imperfect calibration do not mean confidence contains no information. (pp. 37–38.)

Independents improve from 44.7% to 64.6% accuracy. AI-Users improve from 77.2% to 84.8% with correct advice and from 26.8% to 40.6% with faulty advice. Their accurate/faulty gap remains about 44 percentage points under intervention, versus about 50 in control. Usage profiles remain selected behavior. The exploratory three-way interaction involving intervention, trial type, and profile is not significant (p=.534). (pp. 38–40, Figure 9.)

## Interpretation and open question

The authors interpret the improved selectivity as renewed deliberative monitoring. The observable evidence is compatible with that account, but monitoring itself was not directly manipulated or continuously measured. Feedback could change expectations about AI reliability, effort, task knowledge, or all of these. A future factorial study could separately assign incentives and feedback and track how earlier error feedback changes later choices. That is a proposed extension, not an experiment performed here.
